A few weeks ago, in the middle of a difficult week, I realised I needed to step away for a while. I felt tense and had too much on my mind, so I set aside a couple of hours and went alone to a public sauna in Kallio.

For the next two hours, I moved between the heat and the street outside. I would sit in the sauna, cool down, then return for another round, until something in me had settled. I had not solved whatever had made the week difficult, but I left feeling more relaxed and in a better place than when I arrived.

I have come to think of sauna as one of the things I can reach for when my mental health needs some care. I suspect the benefit comes from a combination of things rather than any single cause. Phones stay outside, and nobody is reading a book or watching television. The heat is intense enough to demand my attention, while the lack of other distractions leaves room for my thoughts—or, when I am with a friend, for conversation—and together those things give me space to reset.

## An invitation into the culture

My relationship with sauna did not begin with solitary reflection. When I moved to Finland in 2010, it did not immediately feel like something for me; I found my way into it through other people, going when Finns prompted me as part of the social life I was building here.

I was particularly social during that first summer. I was making new friends, and an evening together would often involve a few beers and a sauna. I went on the cruise ships between Helsinki and Stockholm and used the saunas in both directions; at larger gatherings, there would usually be one somewhere in the plans too. Through those repeated, ordinary visits, sauna gradually became part of my life.

Those early visits were usually separated by gender, and I found sauna to be a levelling experience between men. Sitting naked together in a dark room creates an intimacy quite unlike meeting in a café or pub, but not necessarily an awkward one.

It could also act as an icebreaker. Hearing me speak English often seemed to awaken a certain curiosity in Finns, and conversation would begin around the sauna itself: throwing water onto the stones, enjoying the löyly and sharing the experience. Rather than teaching me how a sauna worked, it felt as though they were inviting me into that part of their culture.

During that first summer, a documentary called [*Miesten vuoro* (*Steam of Life*)](https://www.finna.fi/Record/kavi.elonet_elokuva_1489368) was released. Many of my friends went to see it, but I did not watch it until twelve or thirteen years later.

The film follows Finnish men talking in saunas, broaching difficult subjects and sharing parts of their lives with one another. When I finally watched it, I found it a wonderful reflection of my own experiences of being in sauna with other men. It captured how, on some occasions, the setting can give close friends room to talk about meaningful things in their lives.

## Alone and together

Sauna has since become a significant part of my life. I go several times a month and, depending on what else is happening, sometimes two or three times a week. Much of that is still social.

I meet some of my closest friends at [Kotiharjun Sauna](https://www.kotiharjunsauna.fi/en), a public wood-heated sauna in Kallio that has been there since 1928. It attracts a good mixture of regulars and other locals, with the occasional tourist and foreigner among them, and the atmosphere is usually friendly.

It is also an exceptionally good sauna. The large heat-storing stove keeps the room hot throughout the day, with enough space for a sizeable group without losing the intensity of the heat. Sauna appears in my working life too: I have visited smoke saunas with colleagues, and whenever my company holds an event, there seems to be a sauna at the venue or arranged beforehand.

Time with a friend at Kotiharju follows a simple rhythm. We shower, go into the sauna and talk, then sit outside on the street to cool down and continue the conversation. After a while, we return to the heat, repeating the cycle three, four or sometimes five times.

Sitting outside is one of the things that makes Kotiharju special. You can take a beer, water or soft drink with you and sit on the pavement in a towel while pedestrians walk past. It is a particularly good feeling in winter.

When I go alone, I follow much the same rhythm. I might end up talking to some of the locals, or I might spend the whole visit without interacting with anyone at all. Both feel natural, and that freedom is part of what makes a public sauna work for me.

Of course, a public sauna means sharing the room with people who have arrived for different reasons. Occasionally a large group will come in and make more noise than I would prefer, although this is rare. They are perfectly entitled to enjoy the space in their own way; my desire for a quiet afternoon does not give me ownership of the room.

## Good löyly

Asking what makes good [löyly](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/sauna-culture-in-finland-01596) may be the ultimate question for a Finn. I can only really answer it by describing how it feels.

The sauna is already hot when somebody throws water onto the stones. A wave of heat and moisture moves through the room until it reaches me, bringing the sensation that I am about to become too hot without ever quite getting there. Sometimes it sends shivers across my skin. The wave recedes, the heat settles, and after a while somebody throws more water.

![Water being thrown from a wooden ladle onto the hot stones of a Finnish sauna heater](/things-i-love-sauna-loyly.jpg)

For me, a good sauna maintains that feeling in waves for as long as I am inside. Some people prefer extremely hot saunas and emerge with bright red skin, but I am not interested in treating it as an endurance test. I want to approach the edge of too much without crossing it.

That depends on the sauna being properly heated, the stones retaining the heat and, crucially, people being allowed to throw water onto them. I have visited saunas abroad where there is no water for the stove. They never feel quite right to me.

## A few hours away

Regular sauna bathing has been [associated with lower cardiovascular mortality](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2130724), and I am glad that the physical effects are being studied. They are not how I measure what sauna does for me, though. I notice it most clearly in the difference between how I feel when I arrive and how I feel when I leave.

When I am having a hard time, feeling low or simply need a break, a public sauna gives me somewhere to go without needing company or a plan. For a couple of hours, nothing is expected of me. I can think, talk or sit quietly while the next wave of löyly moves through the room.

After sixteen years in Finland, sauna is thoroughly part of my life. It began as something Finns invited me into and gradually became something I would seek out for myself, whether I wanted time with close friends or time alone.

After the final round, I get dressed and step out of the warm, cosy sauna into Helsinki. The cold air feels especially good in winter, carrying the contrast between the heat inside and the world I am returning to. If I ever left Finland, I know I would start hunting for a sauna that could give me that feeling again.
